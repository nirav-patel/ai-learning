Abstract

Black hole science has entered a golden era of empirical discovery, driven by the convergence of gravitational-wave astronomy, space-based infrared observatories, millimetre-wave interferometry, and increasingly sophisticated theoretical frameworks. This report surveys the most significant confirmed developments from 2024 through mid-2026, encompassing: (1) the completion of LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA's fourth observing run (O4) and the publication of two landmark gravitational-wave transient catalogs (GWTC-4.0 and GWTC-5.0) that bring the total catalog to 390 confirmed events; (2) the detection of GW231123, the most massive binary black hole merger ever observed, challenging pair-instability supernova models; (3) the detection of GW250114, the clearest gravitational-wave signal to date, enabling a 5-sigma confirmation of Hawking's black hole area theorem; (4) new Event Horizon Telescope polarimetric imaging of both M87* and Sgr A*, revealing dynamic magnetic field reversals; (5) JWST's systematic discovery of "overmassive" supermassive black holes in the early universe, upending standard co-evolution models; (6) ongoing theoretical advances concerning the black hole information paradox, firewall problem, and island formula; and (7) progress toward next-generation detectors including ESA's LISA and the Einstein Telescope. Together, these developments represent a fundamental transformation in our observational and theoretical understanding of black holes across all mass scales.

1. Introduction

Black holes — regions of spacetime where gravity is so extreme that not even light can escape — have transitioned from theoretical curiosities to the most observationally rich objects in modern astrophysics. The decade since the first direct detection of gravitational waves on 14 September 2015 (GW150914) has seen the field expand dramatically in both scope and precision. By mid-2026, the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA (LVK) network has catalogued 390 compact binary coalescence events,1 the Event Horizon Telescope has produced multi-epoch polarimetric images of two supermassive black holes,2 and the James Webb Space Telescope has revealed an unexpected population of overmassive black holes in the early universe.3

This report is structured around six thematic areas: (§2) gravitational-wave astronomy and the LVK O4 run; (§3) direct imaging of black hole environments with the EHT; (§4) JWST and early-universe black hole formation; (§5) theoretical advances, including the information paradox and firewall problem; (§6) future facilities and next-generation detectors; and (§7) conclusions. All citations reference primary peer-reviewed literature or official institutional communications. Where specific numerical values — such as signal-to-noise ratios, Bayes factors, or arXiv identifiers — are drawn from preprints rather than final published versions, this is noted explicitly.

A note on scope: this report does not aim to be exhaustive across all of black hole physics. Neutron star–black hole mergers, X-ray binary phenomenology, and stellar-mass black hole demographics are touched upon only where directly relevant to the thematic developments described. Readers seeking comprehensive coverage of those subfields are directed to dedicated review articles cited in the relevant sections.

2. Gravitational-Wave Astronomy: LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA's Fourth Observing Run

2.1 The O4 Observing Run and Catalog Milestones

The fourth observing run (O4) of the LVK network was conducted in two parts: O4a (24 May 2023 – 16 January 2024) and O4b (10 April 2024 – 28 January 2025), totalling approximately 20 months of science-quality data collection. The LVK officially concluded O4 on 18 November 2025, following a period of extended commissioning and data quality review.4

Two landmark catalogs have been published:

GWTC-4.0 (arXiv: 2508.18082, submitted August 2025) covers O4a and adds 128 new compact binary coalescence candidates with astrophysical probability pastro ≥ 0.5, bringing the cumulative catalog to 218 events. Component masses of binary black holes in this catalog range from approximately 5.79 M☉ to 137 M☉, the latter from the extraordinary event GW231123 (discussed in §2.2). For the first time, two signals — GW230814_230901 and GW231226_01520 — achieved network signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) exceeding 30, enabling high-fidelity waveform studies and stringent tests of general relativity.5

GWTC-5.0 (arXiv: 2605.27225, submitted May 2026) covers O4b and adds 161 new candidates, of which 104 have false-alarm rates below 1 yr⁻¹. The cumulative total now stands at 390 confirmed gravitational-wave events. Five binary black hole signals in O4b have network SNR exceeding 30, with GW250114_082203 achieving a record SNR of approximately 76.9 — the clearest gravitational-wave signal ever detected, and more than 30 times above the noise floor.6 Readers should note that GWTC-5.0 was available as a preprint at the time of writing; the final peer-reviewed version may contain revised statistical values.

"This is the most massive black hole binary we've observed through gravitational waves, and it presents a real challenge to our understanding of black hole formation." — Mark Hannam, Cardiff University, on GW231123 (Caltech/LIGO News, 15 July 2025)7

2.2 GW231123: The Most Massive Binary Black Hole Merger Ever Detected

Detected on 23 November 2023 by both LIGO observatories, GW231123 is a landmark event in gravitational-wave astronomy. The LVK Collaboration published the dedicated parameter estimation analysis in July 2025 (arXiv: 2507.08219). The signal is consistent with the merger of two black holes with component masses of approximately 103 M☉ and 137 M☉, producing a remnant black hole of roughly 225 M☉ — firmly in the intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) regime, defined as black holes with masses between approximately 100 and 10⁵ M☉.8

This event is scientifically extraordinary for several interconnected reasons:

The pair-instability mass gap problem. Standard stellar evolution theory predicts that stars with helium core masses of approximately 45–135 M☉ undergo pair-instability supernovae — thermonuclear explosions that completely disrupt the star, leaving no compact remnant. Both components of GW231123 sit squarely within or above this theoretically "forbidden" mass range, in direct tension with single-star evolutionary models.9 The observation therefore demands either a revision of pair-instability physics or an alternative formation channel.

High component spins. Both component black holes are inferred to be rapidly spinning. In the context of binary black hole formation, high spins are a characteristic signature of hierarchical (multi-generation) mergers — that is, black holes that are themselves the products of earlier gravitational-wave-driven mergers rather than direct stellar collapse. First-generation (1G) black holes formed from stellar collapse are expected to have low or moderate spins; the high spins observed in GW231123 are inconsistent with a 1G origin for either component.

Hierarchical merger origin. Li et al. (2025, arXiv: 2507.17551) analysed GW231123 using population-informed Bayesian priors and concluded that both components are most plausibly multi-generation (>2G) merger remnants, consistent with the successive mergers of approximately six and four first-generation black holes, respectively. The preferred formation environment is the accretion disk of an active galactic nucleus (AGN), where gas dynamical friction and the high stellar density can efficiently harden binary orbits and retain merger remnants against gravitational recoil kicks.10

Cosmic string alternative. Cuceu et al. (2025, arXiv: 2507.20778) performed Bayesian model comparison to test whether GW231123 could originate from cusps or kinks on a cosmic string rather than a compact binary merger. Their analysis found strong Bayesian evidence favouring the binary black hole merger hypothesis over the cosmic string model. While Bayesian model comparison yields a probabilistic preference rather than a logical exclusion, the evidence against the cosmic string origin is sufficiently strong that it is not considered a viable explanation for this event.11

2.3 GW250114: Clearest Signal to Date and Hawking's Area Theorem

Detected on 14 January 2025, GW250114_082203 achieved a record network SNR of approximately 76.9 — a signal quality that reflects a decade of incremental detector upgrades since GW150914. The event originated from a binary black hole merger in which both components had masses between approximately 30 and 40 M☉, closely resembling the original 2015 detection in its mass configuration but dramatically surpassing it in signal fidelity.12

The exceptional clarity of GW250114 enabled the most rigorous test of Hawking's black hole area theorem to date. Derived by Stephen Hawking in 1971 from the classical general relativistic framework, this theorem states that the total area of black hole event horizons can never decrease in any physical process, provided the null energy condition holds. For a binary merger, this implies that the surface area of the final remnant black hole must exceed the sum of the areas of the two progenitor black holes. Using the high-SNR waveform of GW250114, the LVK team confirmed this inequality at a confidence level of 99.999% (5σ), a substantial improvement over the 95% confidence achieved in the original area theorem test using GW150914 data (MIT News, 10 September 2025).13

This result is significant not only as a test of classical general relativity but also as a constraint on quantum corrections to black hole thermodynamics: any quantum gravitational effect that causes macroscopic area decrease would have been detectable at this confidence level, and none was observed.

2.4 GW241011 and GW241110: Fast-Spinning Black Holes and Tests of General Relativity

Published on 28 October 2025 in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, the LVK Collaboration reported the detection and analysis of two gravitational-wave events separated by approximately one month in late 2024: GW241011 and GW241110. The primary black hole in GW241011 was measured to be among the fastest-rotating black holes observed to date via gravitational waves, with a dimensionless spin parameter approaching the theoretical Kerr maximum of χ = 1. This measurement places strong constraints on black hole formation channels: rapidly spinning black holes are inconsistent with isolated binary stellar evolution (which tends to produce low-spin remnants) and instead favour dynamical formation in dense stellar environments or hierarchical mergers.

These events also advanced the search for ultralight bosonic particles — including axions and dark photons — that could extract rotational energy from spinning black holes via the superradiance mechanism, a process in which a bosonic field is amplified by extracting energy from the ergosphere. The gravitational-wave data from GW241011 and GW241110 are consistent with standard general relativity and place new upper bounds on the masses of hypothetical beyond-Standard-Model particles in the mass range 10−14–10−12 eV (University of Chicago Physical Sciences Division, 28 October 2025; University of British Columbia Science News, 28 October 2025).14

3. Event Horizon Telescope: New Polarimetric and Multi-Epoch Imaging

3.1 Sagittarius A* in Polarized Light (March 2024)

In March 2024, the EHT Collaboration released the first image of Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*) — the approximately 4 million solar mass black hole at the centre of the Milky Way — in polarized light. The image reveals strongly organised magnetic fields spiralling inward from the vicinity of the black hole's event horizon, strikingly similar in topology to the magnetic field structure previously observed around M87*, despite the two systems differing in mass by more than three orders of magnitude.

According to the EHT team, this morphological similarity suggests that ordered, dynamically important magnetic fields may be a universal feature of accreting supermassive black holes, independent of mass scale. The inferred magnetic field topology is consistent with a magnetically arrested accretion (MAD) state, in which the accumulated magnetic flux at the event horizon is sufficient to periodically arrest the inflow of accreting gas, launching powerful relativistic jets. This result was published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters and announced jointly by the EHT Collaboration, the University of Arizona (news.arizona.edu), and the EHT official website (eventhorizontelescope.org).15

3.2 M87*: Unexpected Polarization Reversals Across Multiple Epochs (September 2025)

In September 2025, the EHT Collaboration released a landmark multi-year study of M87*, comparing observations from 2017, 2018, and 2021. The results, announced at eventhorizontelescope.org and submitted for publication in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, revealed unexpected reversals in the polarization pattern of the magnetic fields surrounding the black hole across observing epochs:

  • In 2017, the magnetic fields exhibited a coherent spiral pattern in one rotational sense.
  • By 2018, the pattern had partially settled into a more ordered configuration.
  • By 2021, the polarization direction had reversed substantially relative to the 2017 baseline.

Critically, while the polarization pattern changed significantly between epochs, the angular diameter of the photon ring remained consistent across all three years — confirming the shadow size predicted by general relativity for a black hole of M87*'s mass (~6.5 × 10⁹ M☉) and providing a stringent multi-epoch test of the Kerr metric. Co-lead Paul Tiede of the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian noted:

"What's remarkable is that while the ring size has remained consistent over the years — confirming the black hole's shadow predicted by Einstein's theory — the polarization pattern changes significantly." — Paul Tiede, Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian16

These polarization reversals most likely reflect dynamical changes in the accretion flow and magnetic field topology on timescales of years, possibly associated with transitions between MAD and standard-and-normal-evolution (SANE) accretion states. They provide the first direct observational evidence for evolving large-scale magnetic structures in the immediate vicinity of a supermassive black hole's event horizon, with important implications for jet launching and variability models.

3.3 EHT Observations of OJ 287: A Binary Supermassive Black Hole Candidate

Published in Astronomy & Astrophysics in January 2026, the first EHT observations of OJ 287 — a blazar long suspected to harbour a binary supermassive black hole system based on its quasi-periodic optical outbursts — provided the highest-resolution radio images of this source to date. The observations were conducted as part of the EHT's expanded source monitoring program and represent a significant step toward directly resolving the putative binary system or its immediate accretion environment.

4. JWST and Early-Universe Black Hole Formation

The James Webb Space Telescope has fundamentally altered our understanding of supermassive black hole formation in the early universe. Prior to JWST, the most distant known quasars — powered by supermassive black holes of ~10⁹ M☉ — were detected at redshifts z ≈ 7, corresponding to a universe less than 800 million years old. Explaining how such massive black holes could form so quickly was already a theoretical challenge; JWST has made that challenge considerably more acute.
